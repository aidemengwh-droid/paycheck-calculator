How Florida Income Tax Works

Florida is one of nine U.S. states that does not levy a state income tax on wages and salaries. The state relies on sales tax and tourism revenue to fund government operations, making it attractive for retirees and high-income earners. Despite having no state income tax, Florida residents still pay federal income tax, Social Security (6.2% on wages up to $184,500 in 2026), and Medicare (1.45%) on their earnings.

The absence of a state income tax means your paycheck in Florida goes further compared to high-tax states like California or New York. For a worker earning $75,000 annually, living in Florida instead of California could save thousands of dollars per year in state taxes alone. However, Florida may offset this through other taxes such as sales tax (currently 6% (plus local)) or property tax.

Federal Taxes for Florida Residents

All Florida residents pay federal income tax using the same 2026 federal tax brackets, regardless of which state they live in. The federal standard deduction for 2026 is $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married filing jointly, and $24,150 for head of household. Federal tax rates range from 10% to 37% across seven brackets.

In addition to federal income tax, all workers pay FICA taxes: Social Security at 6.2% (capped at $184,500 in 2026) and Medicare at 1.45%. High earners also pay an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on wages above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married). These federal taxes are the same no matter which state you live in.

How Florida Compares to Other States

Florida ranks among the most tax-friendly states for income earners, joining Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming in having no state income tax. If you are considering relocating for tax reasons, Florida offers significant savings compared to high-tax states like California (up to 13.3%), New York (up to 10.9%), or Hawaii (up to 10%).

Tips for Florida Taxpayers

To maximize your take-home pay in Florida, consider the following strategies:

  • Adjust your W-4 withholdings to match your actual tax liability. Over-withholding gives the government an interest-free loan; under-withholding can result in penalties.
  • Maximize retirement contributions to a 401(k) or IRA. While Florida has no state income tax, these contributions still reduce your federal taxable income.
  • Review your filing status annually. Marriage, divorce, or having dependents can significantly impact your tax situation.
  • Track deductible expenses throughout the year, especially if you plan to itemize deductions instead of taking the standard deduction.
